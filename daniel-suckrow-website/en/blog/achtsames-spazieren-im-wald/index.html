Mindful walking in the forest: Seven ideas for truly arriving

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

“The journey is the destination.” This thought, often attributed to Confucius, fits a walk in the forest beautifully. Many of us do go outside regularly, but in our minds we are still at work or already at the next appointment. This article shows why the forest can do us so much good and gives you seven ideas from my own practice that turn your next walk into a real break.

Why the forest does us so much good

In Japan there is even a special term for it: shinrin-yoku, translated as “forest bathing”. It means nothing more than walking slowly through the forest with all your senses. Studies show that even a short stay in green spaces can lower pulse and blood pressure, reduce the stress hormone cortisol and improve mood.

A large study from England with almost 20,000 people found an interesting threshold: those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature felt considerably healthier and more satisfied than people who hardly went outside. It made no difference whether the time was spent in one go or spread over several short outings.

Why this is so has not yet been fully explained. Several things probably work together: movement, fresh air, daylight, a break from noise and screens and the gentle, undirected attention that nature awakens in us. The forest is no substitute for treatment, but it is a valuable addition for more calm in everyday life.

Seven ideas for your next walk

You do not have to try all the ideas at once. Pick one or two that appeal to you.

  • Take time without a goal. You do not have to cover a distance or keep a pace. Stop when something catches your interest. If you have an appointment later, set a gentle alarm so you do not keep looking at the clock.
  • Look consciously. Notice colours, light and shadow, the patterns in the bark, moss and leaves. You do not have to name or judge anything. Just let your gaze wander.
  • Use all your senses. Listen to birdsong, wind and rustling. Smell the damp earth or the scent after rain. Feel the air on your skin.
  • Look at a small detail. Pick a leaf, a stone or a piece of bark and look at it closely for a minute. Then leave it where it was.
  • Feel the ground. If the surface allows, you can walk a stretch barefoot or in barefoot shoes. That way you notice differences in temperature and texture more intensely.
  • Walk with respect. Stay on the paths, watch animals from a distance and take your rubbish home. The forest is not just a backdrop for our wellbeing, but a habitat for countless species.
  • Sense inwards. How does your body feel? Is your breathing calm? Where are you holding tension? You do not have to change anything, just notice.

Important: Only walk barefoot on clear, clean ground. With open wounds, diabetes or circulation and nerve conditions in the feet, it is better to avoid it. In areas with many ticks, closed shoes and long clothing protect you. Do not collect or taste plants or mushrooms you cannot identify with certainty.

What to do when your thoughts wander?

They will, constantly. That is completely normal and not a sign that you are doing something wrong. Mindfulness does not mean thinking of nothing. It means noticing where your attention is right now and then kindly bringing it back.

A simple anchor is your steps. Feel how your heel touches down, your foot rolls and lifts off the ground again. You can also count along silently: breathe in for four steps, breathe out for four steps. As soon as you notice that you are back at your shopping list, simply return to your steps. Each return is the actual practice.

A small exercise to take along

The 5-4-3-2-1 exercise

Stop at a spot where you feel comfortable and take three deep breaths. Then notice, one after the other: five things you see, four things you hear, three things you feel on your skin or under your feet, two things you smell and one thing you are grateful for right now. The exercise only takes two to three minutes and reliably brings you out of the thought carousel and back into the moment.

Tip: If possible, leave your phone in flight mode or in your pocket. If you want to take a photo, take it consciously and then put the phone away again.

How to make it a habit

You benefit most when the walk becomes a fixed ritual. It does not have to be an hours-long hike. Just 20 to 30 minutes, three to four times a week, gets you to the 120 minutes of nature that were linked to greater wellbeing in studies.

  • Link the walk to an existing habit, for example after lunch or straight after work.
  • Choose a route you can reach quickly. A park or a path along a river also works.
  • Go in any weather. Rain, fog and snow change the forest and make every walk different.
  • Take someone along now and then and agree to walk the first ten minutes in silence.

Conclusion

A mindful walk in the forest is one of the simplest ways to reduce stress and come back to yourself. Studies show that just 120 minutes of nature a week is linked to greater wellbeing. Take time without a goal, use all your senses and kindly return when your thoughts wander. Treat the forest with respect as you do so. You need no equipment and no prior knowledge, just the willingness to slow down.
