Mindful walking in the forest: consciously perceive the moment

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

“The journey is the destination” – a thought often attributed to Confucius that fits well with a mindful walk. Our everyday lives are often characterized by appointments, obligations and time pressure. A nearby forest can become an easily accessible place where we can consciously slow down. Staying in nature can support well-being and be associated with a reduction in stress. However, they are not a substitute for necessary medical or psychotherapeutic treatment. The following suggestions are based on personal experiences and are intended to invite you to discover your own approach to the forest.

1. Walk barefoot or with barefoot shoes

If you can feel the surface directly or with a thin sole, you can be more aware of differences in soil structure, temperature and condition. This can promote body awareness and attention to each step.

Safety is important: only walk barefoot on clear, clean and suitable surfaces. Roots, thorns, broken glass, sharp stones and animal droppings can cause injuries. If you have open wounds, reduced feeling in your feet or certain circulatory and nerve diseases, you should avoid going barefoot or seek medical advice beforehand. In tick-rich areas, closed shoes and long clothing are safer protection.

2. Look consciously

We see things all the time, but we don't always consciously perceive them. Often our minds are already on the next appointment or busy with something that isn't here at the moment. So try to slow down your gaze for a moment.

Notice colors, light and shadows, patterns in the tree bark, moss, leaves and small movements. You don't have to rate or name anything. Just let your eyes wander and discover details that you might otherwise miss.

3. Don’t set a fixed time limit

If possible, let go of the time pressure for a while. You don't have to complete a specific route or maintain a pace. If something interests you, stand still or sit on a bench.

If you have an appointment afterwards, a gentle alarm clock can help. This way you don't have to constantly look at the clock and you can still go back in time. Just a few conscious minutes can be a small interruption to everyday life.

4. Involve all senses

The forest cannot only be experienced with the eyes. Listen to bird calls, wind and the rustling of leaves. Notice smells - such as damp earth, plants or the scent of a summer rain. Feel the temperature on your skin and the ground beneath your feet.

You can also pause for a moment and observe how the sounds change as you become quiet. Pay attention to your surroundings and avoid tasting plants or mushrooms: many species are poisonous or cannot be identified with certainty without specialist knowledge.

5. Look more closely at an object

Find something inconspicuous on the side of the path - for example a leaf, a stone or a piece of bark - and look at it for a while. What shapes, lines, colors and structures do you recognize? You may discover small feeding marks, moss or other details.

Leave the item where it is. Dead wood, leaves, fungi and other natural components are important habitats and part of the ecosystem. You can discover a lot without taking anything with you or changing anything.

6. Treat the forest with respect

The forest is a habitat for numerous plants, animals and fungi and fulfills important ecological functions. We humans also depend on healthy ecosystems. A mindful walk can therefore also be an opportunity to recognize your own connection to nature.

If possible, stay on the paths, avoid unnecessary noise and do not leave trash behind. Observe animals from a distance and respect protected areas and local rules. Treating it with respect also means viewing the forest not just as a backdrop for your own well-being, but as an independent living space.

7. Mindfulness: Noticing what is happening within you

From time to time, direct your attention inwards. How does your body feel? Is your breathing calm or rather rapid? Do you feel tension in your shoulders or jaw? What thoughts or feelings arise as you walk?

You don't have to force anything and you don't have to reach a certain state of relaxation. When your mind wanders, you can gently return to the awareness of your footsteps, your breathing, or the sounds. Maybe you notice your mood changing - maybe not. Both are fine.

A little exercise for your next walk in the forest

Try a simple sequence for five minutes:

  • Walk slowly at first and consciously feel every step.
  • Stop briefly and notice three sounds.
  • Then find three colors or shapes that you didn't notice before.
  • Breathe calmly and naturally a few times without forcing your breath.
  • End the exercise by noticing how you feel right now.

There is no right or wrong. Mindfulness does not mean emptying your mind completely, but rather continually paying attention to the present moment.

Conclusion: The forest does not have to be another destination

A mindful walk in the forest does not require any special equipment or a specific route. Sometimes it's enough to slow down, look more closely and perceive your surroundings with multiple senses. Use the suggestions that feel good and safe for you and develop your own little routine from them.
