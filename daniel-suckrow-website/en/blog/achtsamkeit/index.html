Mindfulness in everyday life: What it does and how to start without a meditation cushion

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Many days feel like a race from one task to the next. In the evening you wonder where the time went and what you actually ate. Mindfulness invites you to pause now and then and really be present. This article shows you what lies behind it, what research says and how to start without a meditation cushion.

What mindfulness means and what it does not

Mindfulness means consciously perceiving what is happening right now, without judging it immediately: your thoughts, feelings, physical sensations and your surroundings. It is not about emptying your mind or being relaxed all the time. It is about noticing what is there and kindly bringing your attention back to the moment again and again.

Nor does mindfulness mean living only in the moment. Planning, remembering and learning from experience are important. But mindfulness helps you not to get permanently lost in brooding over the past or worrying about the future.

It became known above all through the MBSR programme, short for “Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction”. The molecular biologist Jon Kabat-Zinn developed it in the USA at the end of the 1970s. It combines meditation, body awareness and gentle yoga in an eight-week course.

What research shows

Mindfulness programmes are among the best-studied relaxation methods. Reviews show that they can reduce stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms in many people. The effects are mostly small to moderate and similar to those of other proven methods such as exercise or relaxation techniques. In recurrent depression, a special mindfulness programme can lower the risk of relapse.

How much you benefit depends on how regularly you practise. A few minutes a day often achieve more than one long session a month.

Important: Mindfulness is not a cure-all and does not replace therapy for mental illness. Sometimes difficult feelings or memories also come up when you pause. If you have had traumatic experiences or the exercises put a heavy strain on you, it is best to practise with professional support.

Mindful eating: less on the side, more enjoyment

As a nutrition consultant, one area is particularly close to my heart: mindful eating. Many of us eat on the side, in front of a screen, on the go or under time pressure. In the process we hardly notice what and how much we eat and when we are actually full.

Mindful eating can help you perceive hunger and fullness better again, recognise emotional eating and enjoy meals more. It is not a diet, but an attitude:

  • Eat at least one meal a day without your phone, TV or laptop.
  • Pause briefly before eating. How hungry are you right now on a scale from one to ten?
  • Consciously notice colours, smells and taste. Chew slowly and put your cutlery down now and then.
  • Check halfway through how full you already are. The fullness signal takes about 20 minutes.
  • When cravings hit, ask yourself: am I really hungry, or stressed, tired or bored?

Five small exercises for everyday life

You need neither a cushion nor incense for mindfulness. These exercises fit into any day:

  • The one-minute pause: feel your feet touching the floor. Observe three breaths without changing them. Notice what you hear.
  • The mindful coffee or tea: drink your first drink of the morning without your phone. Feel the warmth of the cup and smell consciously.
  • The door-handle anchor: take one conscious breath every time you open a door.
  • Thought labels: when you are brooding, name silently what is happening, for example “worrying” or “planning”. This creates some distance.
  • The evening review: before falling asleep, recall three moments of the day that were good, even if they were small.

A practical example

You usually eat lunch quickly at your desk and have cravings for sweets in the afternoon. For two weeks you try the following: you eat lunch without a screen, take three calm breaths before the first bite and briefly put your cutlery down halfway through. Many people notice after just a few days that they feel full sooner and their afternoon cravings ease.

Three common misunderstandings

  • “I can’t meditate because I think too much.” That is exactly what the practice is for. It is not about having no thoughts, but about noticing them.
  • “I don’t have time for this.” Even one minute counts. Mindfulness can be built into activities you do anyway, such as showering, washing up or commuting.
  • “Mindfulness means always being calm.” Anger, sadness or restlessness are allowed to be there too. Mindfulness helps you notice them without being swept away by them straight away.

How to keep it up

The biggest mistake is to start too big. Begin with two to five minutes a day and link the exercise to something you do anyway, such as brushing your teeth or your first coffee. Apps or guided meditations can help at the beginning. If you want to go deeper, you can find MBSR courses in many cities, some of which are subsidised by health insurers in Germany.

And if you forget a day: no problem. Kindly starting again is part of the practice too.

Conclusion

Mindfulness means consciously perceiving what is there, without judging it right away. Studies show that regular practice can ease stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms. But it is no substitute for therapy. Mindful eating is particularly practical for everyday life: without a screen, slowly and with attention to hunger and fullness. Start small, with a few minutes a day, and link the exercises to fixed habits.
