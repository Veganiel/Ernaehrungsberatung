Mindfulness: Become more aware of the moment

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Everyday life is often full of tasks, stimuli and expectations. This can easily give you the feeling that you can only function from one appointment to the next. Mindfulness invites you to pause for a moment and become more aware of your own experience - without having to immediately evaluate or change it.

What does mindfulness mean?

Mindfulness describes conscious, as open as possible attention to what is currently happening: thoughts, feelings, physical sensations and the environment. It's not about clearing your mind completely or suppressing unpleasant thoughts. Rather, it's about noticing what is there and always bringing your attention back to the present moment in a friendly way.

The past and future are important parts of our thinking: we remember, learn from experiences, plan and prepare. Mindfulness does not mean devaluing these skills or just living in the moment. Rather, it can help you not to permanently lose yourself in ruminations about the past or worries about the future.

Why can mindfulness be helpful?

Mindfulness-based programs have been examined in numerous studies. Systematic reviews show that they can reduce stress and anxiety symptoms to a small to moderate extent in some people. There is also evidence of positive effects for certain areas of attention and cognitive performance.

However, the results are not the same for all people and depend, among other things, on the type, duration and quality of the exercise. Mindfulness is not a panacea and does not replace professional support for psychological problems. However, it can be a helpful supplement to practice dealing with thoughts, feelings and stresses.

Practice mindfulness in everyday life

There is no one right way. Some people meditate, others practice yoga, go for walks or consciously take time to notice their surroundings while riding the bus. What is important is not the perfect technique, but rather finding a form of attention that can be integrated into your own everyday life.

A simple exercise: Pause for a minute. Feel your feet touch the ground. Notice how you breathe without having to change your breath. Listen to the sounds around you and notice what thoughts are arising. When you wander, notice it kindly and return to awareness.

11 impulses for more mindfulness

1. Notice the thought carousel: Don't try to stop thoughts by force. Recognize that you are ruminating and gently return your attention.

2. Don't immediately evaluate thoughts: Notice what's going through your mind without treating each thought as fact or a call to action.

3. Notice the breath: Focus your attention on inhaling and exhaling for a few breaths. You don't have to control your breath.

4. Create small rituals: Start the day consciously, drink your tea without looking at your cell phone or take a moment of peace before going to bed.

5. Reduce distractions: Put your smartphone aside for certain periods of time and consciously create small breaks without digital stimuli.

6. Create a pleasant environment: Nature, light, sounds or scents can help you consciously engage with your surroundings.

7. Write down thoughts: If something is bothering you, write it down. This way you can come back to it later instead of getting lost in it over and over again in the moment.

8. Use your senses: As you walk, pay attention to wind, light, sounds, colors or the contact of your feet with the ground.

9. Observe instead of immediately judging: Try to perceive a situation as accurately as possible before evaluating it.

10. Be creative: Painting, playing music or designing can be an opportunity to dedicate yourself to an activity in depth. It doesn't have to be a perfect result.

11. Stay curious: Look consciously and discover things that you often overlook in your everyday life.

An important note: mindfulness does not always have to be pleasant

For some people, silent meditation or intense concentration on internal sensations can increase unpleasant feelings. If a practice becomes stressful, it is okay to stop, turn your attention outward, or choose another form of practice. If psychological problems persist, professional support is useful.

Conclusion: Small moments make the difference

Mindfulness does not have to mean meditating for a long time every day or fundamentally changing everyday life. Even brief moments of conscious awareness can help you pause and notice your own experience more clearly. Maybe it starts with leaving your phone in your pocket next time you go for a walk and really noticing what's going on around you for a few minutes.
