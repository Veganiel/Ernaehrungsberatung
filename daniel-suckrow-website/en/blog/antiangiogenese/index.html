Antiangiogenesis and cancer: What nutrition can actually do

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Tumours need blood vessels to grow. This insight changed cancer medicine. Nowadays you often read that certain foods could “starve” tumours in the same way. This article shows what lies behind the term anti-angiogenesis, what research on foods really says and which diet has been shown to lower your cancer risk.

What is angiogenesis?

Angiogenesis means the formation of new blood vessels from existing ones. At first, this is a completely normal and vital process. Your body needs it, for example, for wound healing, building muscle and during pregnancy.

Angiogenesis is controlled by a finely tuned balance of promoting and inhibiting messenger substances. One of the most important promoting messengers is called VEGF, short for “vascular endothelial growth factor”. Normally these signals are well balanced, and new vessels only form where they are needed.

Why tumours need new blood vessels

Without its own blood supply, a tumour can only grow to a size of a few millimetres. After that it lacks oxygen and nutrients. Many tumours therefore send out signals such as VEGF and get the surrounding tissue to form new vessels towards them.

As early as 1971, the American surgeon Judah Folkman presented the idea of “starving” tumours by blocking this formation of new vessels. For a long time he was ridiculed for it. Today his approach is considered one of the important developments in modern cancer medicine.

Anti-angiogenesis in medicine

Since 2004 there have been medicines that specifically target the formation of new vessels. The antibody bevacizumab, for example, captures VEGF. Other agents block the receptors for these signals in the vessel cells. They are used in bowel, kidney, lung and ovarian cancer, among others, usually in combination with other therapies.

Anti-angiogenesis has also changed a lot in eye medicine. In wet age-related macular degeneration, abnormal vessels grow under the retina. Injections with VEGF inhibitors can preserve eyesight here.

Experience also shows, however, that anti-angiogenesis alone does not usually cure cancer. Tumours often find ways around the blockade. The medicines extend life in certain types of cancer, but they are not a miracle cure.

Can foods starve tumours?

Books and talks repeatedly name foods that are supposed to inhibit angiogenesis. These include green tea, turmeric, berries, tomatoes, soya, broccoli or garlic. Some of their ingredients do show such effects in the lab, for example EGCG from green tea, genistein from soya, curcumin from turmeric or sulforaphane from broccoli.

There are, however, three important limitations:

  • Most results come from cell cultures or animal experiments, not from studies in humans.
  • The lab often uses concentrations that you would never reach in your blood through food.
  • Whether a food actually protects humans against cancer via the formation of new vessels has not been proven.

Important: Diet does not replace cancer treatment. You should only take high-dose extracts of green tea, turmeric or other plants during therapy after consulting your treatment team. Green tea extract can, for example, weaken the effect of the cancer drug bortezomib, and some extracts put a strain on the liver.

How to assess health promises

Especially when it comes to cancer, you come across many headlines about “cancer-fighting” foods. These questions help you assess them better:

  • Was the effect shown in humans, or only in cells and animals?
  • Was it about a food in normal amounts or a high-dose extract?
  • Was a disease actually prevented, or was only a lab value changed?
  • Do several independent studies confirm the result?
  • Does the source earn money from a book, course or supplement on the topic?

If the first four questions are answered with “only in the lab”, “extract”, “lab value” and “no”, caution is advised. That does not mean the research is worthless. It is simply not yet far enough along to derive recommendations from it.

What you can really do for your cancer risk

Even if individual foods are not miracle cures, diet as a whole certainly is important. The World Cancer Research Fund has evaluated thousands of studies. These factors have been shown to lower the risk of many types of cancer:

  • Plenty of vegetables, fruit, pulses and wholegrains. Fibre, for example, significantly lowers the risk of bowel cancer.
  • Little red meat and as little sausage or processed meat as possible.
  • As little alcohol as possible. Even small amounts increase the risk of breast, bowel and other cancers.
  • A healthy body weight and regular exercise.
  • Few highly processed foods and sugary drinks.

These recommendations work through many mechanisms at once: less inflammation, a healthier microbiome, more stable blood sugar and a better hormone balance. It is quite possible that angiogenesis plays a role here too.

A practical example

Instead of buying expensive turmeric or green tea capsules, you drink one to two cups of green tea a day, season your lentil curry with turmeric and black pepper and eat berries, tomatoes and cabbage vegetables every day. That way you use the phytochemicals in natural amounts and benefit from fibre and many other nutrients at the same time.

Diet during cancer

If you are ill yourself, diet has different priorities. Often the main aim is to maintain weight and muscle, ease side effects and take in enough energy and protein. Strict diets or trying to “starve” the tumour through food do not make sense and can even do harm.

So always discuss nutrition questions with your treatment team. Many clinics offer oncological nutrition counselling.

Conclusion

Blocking the formation of new vessels is an important approach in modern cancer medicine. For individual foods there are interesting signs in the lab, but no evidence in humans that they starve tumours this way. What has been shown to help is a healthy overall package: plenty of vegetables, fruit, pulses and wholegrains, little meat and alcohol, exercise and a healthy weight. Diet can accompany cancer treatment, but never replace it.
