Fibre: Why your gut needs it and how to reach 30 grams

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Fibre is one of the most underrated parts of our diet. It keeps your digestion moving, feeds the good bacteria in your gut and has been shown to protect against many diseases. This article explains exactly what fibre does, how much you need and how to reach 30 grams a day with ease.

What is fibre anyway?

Fibre is the part of plant foods that your small intestine cannot digest. It therefore reaches the large intestine largely unchanged. The German word for it means “ballast”, which sounds like something superfluous. In fact, it is anything but.

There are two groups that complement each other:

  • Soluble fibre such as beta-glucan from oats or pectin from apples binds water and forms a gel. It keeps you full for a long time, slows the rise in blood sugar and can lower LDL cholesterol.
  • Insoluble fibre such as cellulose from wholegrains and vegetables swells and increases stool volume. This stimulates digestion and prevents constipation.

Most plant foods contain both types. That is why variety is worth more than any single “superfood”.

What fibre does in your gut

Trillions of bacteria live in your large intestine. Many of them feed on fibre. When they break it down, short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate are produced. They are the most important energy source for the cells of the gut lining and strengthen the gut barrier.

The more varied your fibre sources, the more varied your microbiome usually becomes. And a diverse microbiome is considered a sign of a healthy gut.

The research is clear as well: a large analysis commissioned by the WHO showed that people eating 25 to 29 grams of fibre a day had a 15 to 30 per cent lower risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer than people who ate little of it. Even higher amounts were linked to an even greater benefit.

Resistant starch and psyllium husks

Resistant starch plays a special role. It forms when cooked potatoes, pasta or spelt grains cool down. Part of the starch changes its structure in the process and is no longer digested in the small intestine. In the large intestine it then acts like fibre and serves as food for your gut bacteria. Green bananas and pulses also contain resistant starch.

In practice this means: potato salad, a pasta salad from the day before or reheated spelt grains are good additions. Most of the resistant starch is retained even when reheated.

Psyllium husks are a popular aid when digestion is sluggish. They bind a great deal of water and can help well with constipation. However, they are an addition and not a substitute for high-fibre foods. Always take them with plenty of fluid.

How much fibre do you need?

The German Nutrition Society (DGE) recommends at least 30 grams of fibre a day for adults. Most people in Germany do not reach this value. On average, intake is about 20 to 25 grams.

With a varied plant-based diet, you usually reach 35 to 50 grams all by yourself. The following table shows how quickly the amount adds up.

How to reach 30 grams in everyday life

You do not have to change your whole diet for this. A few simple habits are often enough:

  • Start the day with oats, berries and a spoonful of flaxseed.
  • Replace white-flour products with wholegrain bread, wholegrain pasta or spelt grains.
  • Plan pulses at least once a day, for example lentils, beans or chickpeas.
  • Fill half your plate with vegetables.
  • For snacks, reach for nuts, fruit or vegetable sticks with hummus.

A practical example

For breakfast you have 50 grams of oats with raspberries and a tablespoon of flaxseed. That is already around 14 grams. At lunch you eat two slices of wholegrain bread with hummus and vegetables, which adds about 10 grams. In the evening there is a lentil dal with broccoli, another 15 grams or so. That way you easily end up at almost 40 grams.

Increase slowly and drink enough

If you have eaten little fibre so far, increase the amount step by step over two to three weeks. Your gut and its bacteria need some time to adjust. Mild bloating at the beginning is normal and usually settles by itself.

Also drink enough, ideally 1.5 to 2 litres of water or unsweetened tea a day. Fibre binds water. Without enough fluid it can even slow digestion down.

Tip: Pulses become easier to digest if you soak them well, pour away the soaking water and cook them with spices such as cumin, fennel or ginger.

Important: With irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease or after bowel surgery, an adjusted fibre intake may be necessary. It is best to discuss this with your doctor or a nutrition professional.

Conclusion

Fibre is not ballast but food for your gut. It keeps digestion moving, keeps you full for longer and lowers the risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and bowel cancer. The goal is at least 30 grams a day. With pulses, wholegrains, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds you will reach it easily. Increase the amount slowly and drink enough, and your gut will quickly get used to it.
