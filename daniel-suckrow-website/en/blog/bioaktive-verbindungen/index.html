Creatine, taurine, carnitine and co.: Is anything missing on a plant-based diet?

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Creatine, carnosine, taurine and carnitine are mainly found in meat and fish. Critics of plant-based diets therefore like to cite them as proof that something is missing without animal foods. This article shows what your body can make itself, when a supplement really makes sense and why choline deserves the most attention.

Made, not eaten

All five substances in this article have one thing in common: your body can make them wholly or partly itself. The building blocks are amino acids from protein, which you also get from pulses, wholegrains, nuts and seeds.

On a plant-based diet you take in much less of them through food. That is why the stores or blood levels of some substances are somewhat lower. But that does not automatically mean a deficiency. What matters is whether this affects health or performance. That is exactly what we will now look at, substance by substance.

Creatine: the most useful supplement

Creatine is a fast energy store for muscles and the brain. It helps to replenish the cells’ energy currency, ATP, at lightning speed during short, intense efforts. Your body makes creatine in the liver and kidneys from the amino acids arginine, glycine and methionine, about 1 gram per day. On a mixed diet another gram comes from meat and fish.

People who eat plant-based therefore often have somewhat lower creatine stores in their muscles. Supplementing with creatine monohydrate is one of the best-studied supplements of all. It can improve performance in strength training and repeated sprints. Early studies also suggest that people who eat plant-based in particular could benefit mentally too, for example in memory.

  • 3 to 5 grams of creatine monohydrate per day have proven themselves.
  • A loading phase with higher amounts is not necessary. The stores are full after about three to four weeks.
  • Creatine is naturally free of animal ingredients, as it is produced synthetically.

Important: If you have kidney disease, you should only take creatine after consulting a doctor. Creatine also slightly raises the lab value creatinine without the kidneys being damaged. It is best to mention this at a blood test.

Carnosine and beta-alanine: only for certain sports

Carnosine buffers acid in the muscles that builds up during intense exercise. Your body builds it from the amino acids beta-alanine and histidine. The limiting building block is beta-alanine.

A beta-alanine supplement can increase carnosine stores and improve performance in intense efforts lasting about one to four minutes, for example in rowing, middle-distance running or martial arts. For everyday life or as an anti-ageing agent there is no proven benefit. A temporary tingling on the skin can occur as a harmless side effect.

Choline: the substance that deserves attention

Choline is needed for cell membranes, fat metabolism in the liver and the messenger acetylcholine in the nervous system. Your body can only make small amounts of it itself. That makes choline the most important of the five substances to take in through your diet.

EFSA gives an adequate intake of 400 milligrams per day for adults, 480 during pregnancy and 520 milligrams while breastfeeding. Eggs and meat are the best-known sources. On a plant-based diet you get on well with these foods:

  • Soya beans, tofu, tempeh and soya drink
  • Pulses such as chickpeas, beans and lentils
  • Cruciferous vegetables such as broccoli, Brussels sprouts and cauliflower
  • Quinoa, wheat germ, peanuts and mushrooms

During pregnancy, choline is particularly important for the child’s development. Here it is worth taking a close look at your diet and, if necessary, a pregnancy supplement that contains choline.

Taurine and carnitine: usually no need to act

Taurine is a sulphur-containing compound found in the heart, brain, muscles and eyes. Your body makes it from the amino acids methionine and cysteine, with the help of vitamin B6. Somewhat lower taurine levels have been measured in people who eat plant-based. However, no health disadvantage has been proven.

Carnitine transports fatty acids into the power plants of the cells, the mitochondria. Your body makes it from lysine and methionine, with the help of vitamin C, iron and B vitamins. In healthy adults, this own production is sufficient, even if hardly any carnitine is taken in through food. Interestingly, gut bacteria turn carnitine from meat into a substance called TMAO, which has been linked to cardiovascular disease.

A practical example

You eat plant-based and do strength training three times a week. Then you take 3 grams of creatine monohydrate a day, for example in your breakfast muesli or in a shake. You ensure choline with tofu, lentils and regular broccoli. You do not need to buy taurine, carnitine or carnosine separately.

Conclusion

On a plant-based diet you are generally not missing anything important with these substances, because your body can largely make them itself. The one exception with real added value is creatine: 3 to 5 grams a day can support strength, performance and possibly mental performance too. You should plan choline deliberately via soya, pulses and cabbage vegetables. For taurine, carnitine and carnosine there is no reason for a supplement in healthy people.
