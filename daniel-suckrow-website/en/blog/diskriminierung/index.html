Discrimination and animals: Why sentience matters

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

When they hear the word discrimination, most people think of people who are disadvantaged because of their origin, gender or body. Animal ethics asks a broader question: is it fair to give less weight to a living being’s interests simply because it belongs to a different species? This article invites you to reflect on this and links the ethical question with facts about the climate and world food supply.

What animals can feel

Hardly anyone today disputes that animals feel pain. But research shows even more: many animals can experience fear, stress, joy, curiosity and social bonds. Pigs solve tasks at the level of small children, chickens recognise dozens of their peers, and cows form close relationships with their calves.

In 2012, leading neuroscientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness. It states that all mammals and birds, as well as many other animals, possess the neurological basis for conscious experience. In 2024, researchers affirmed in the New York Declaration that there is serious evidence of sentience in fish, octopuses and some insects as well.

What speciesism means

The psychologist Richard Ryder coined the term speciesism in 1970. The philosopher Peter Singer made it widely known in 1975. It means giving less weight to a living being’s interests solely because of its species, similar to how an arbitrary characteristic decides treatment in racism or sexism.

The idea behind it is simple: if we consider suffering in humans morally important, we have to justify why comparable suffering in animals should count for less. That does not mean humans and animals must be treated the same in every respect. A pig does not need the right to vote. But its interest in not suffering pain is very similar to that of a dog or a human.

How far this analogy holds is answered differently in philosophy. There are approaches that mainly aim to avoid suffering, approaches that grant animals rights of their own and those that focus on the relationship between humans and animals. What they have in common is that they demand a justification where we have often simply acted out of habit.

A thought experiment

Food for thought

Imagine you learn that in a neighbouring country, dogs are kept under the same conditions as pigs in many barns: in a confined space, with no outdoor access, slaughtered after a few months. Most people would be outraged. What exactly distinguishes the dog from the pig so that we accept one and not the other? Is it a characteristic of the animal, or mainly our habit?

Questions like these are uncomfortable. They are not meant to make anyone feel guilty. But they help you consciously examine your own standards instead of simply adopting them.

Diet, climate and world food supply

The ethical question does not only concern the animals themselves. Worldwide, more than 80 billion land animals are slaughtered for food every year. A large analysis in the journal Science showed that animal foods provide only about 18 per cent of the world’s calories and 37 per cent of its protein, yet take up around 83 per cent of farmland.

A large part of the world’s soya harvest, by the way, ends up in animal feed, not in tofu. If more plant foods were eaten directly by people, considerably more people could be fed on the same land.

Hunger has many causes, however, including poverty, wars and lack of access to land. And not every plant product is automatically sustainable. Cultivation, water use and transport also play a role. In some regions, grazing uses land on which no arable farming is possible. On average, though, a plant-focused diet clearly comes out ahead.

How to form your own opinion

On this topic, facts and values often get mixed up. It helps to separate the two:

  • Facts can be checked: how much land does a food need? What do we know about pain perception in fish?
  • Values have to be justified by each person: how much weight do I give to animals’ interests? Which compromises do I consider acceptable?
  • Ask yourself whether you apply the same standards to humans and animals and, if not, why not.
  • Talk to people who think differently. Good arguments also stand up to counter-questions.

Important: This is not about perfection or judging others. Every step that reduces unnecessary suffering counts. Some people cut down on animal products, others leave them out completely. What matters is that your decision fits your own, well-considered values.

Conclusion

Many animals are sentient and can experience pain, fear and joy. This raises the question of why their interests should count for less solely because of their species. There is no simple answer, but there is a duty to justify. At the same time, the data show that a plant-focused diet causes considerably less land use and fewer emissions on average. Anyone who keeps animals, the climate and global justice in mind has good reasons to put more plant foods on their plate.
