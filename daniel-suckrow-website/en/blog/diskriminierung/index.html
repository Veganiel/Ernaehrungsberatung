Ethics

Discrimination: When bodies are valued and interests are overlooked

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Discrimination is often discussed in the context of human groups. However, an ethical consideration can also ask how we treat non-human animals and what criteria we use to take their interests into account. This article examines sentience, moral equality, and the resource consequences of different food systems.

Sensitivity as a morally relevant criterion

Many non-human animals are sentient and can experience pain, stress, and positive and negative states. From an ethical perspective, the question arises as to whether their interests should be given less weight simply because of their species affiliation.

The term speciesism describes the discrimination against living beings solely because of their membership in a particular species. It is discussed in animal ethics as an analogy to other forms of arbitrary unequal treatment. Whether and in what form this analogy is valid is a philosophical question and should be justified argumentatively.

Food, Agriculture and Global Resources

Food production uses land, water and energy and influences biodiversity and greenhouse gas emissions. The environmental impacts vary greatly depending on food, production method and region. On average, many animal foods - especially products from ruminants - require more land and cause higher greenhouse gas emissions per kilogram of protein than many plant-based alternatives.

Some of the global arable land is used for animal feed. Greater use of plant-based foods for direct human consumption can reduce land requirements under certain conditions. However, it does not follow that every plant-based product is automatically ecologically superior: cultivation practices, transportation, water scarcity and local ecosystems also play a role.

Hunger arises not only from the number of calories available, but also from poverty, conflict, political instability and lack of access to land and infrastructure, among other things. Livestock farming in certain regions may use by-products or land that is not suitable for agriculture; Intensive feed production, on the other hand, sometimes competes with the cultivation of food.

Apply moral standards consistently

A central question is which properties constitute moral consideration. If the ability to suffer is considered relevant in humans, it must be explained why comparable sentience in animals should be assessed fundamentally differently. This does not automatically result in a single ethical position, but rather an obligation to justify different moral standards.

A reflected discussion should distinguish empirical statements – for example about animal husbandry or resource balance – from normative statements about rights, duties and moral status.

Conclusion

Addressing discrimination and nutrition leads to questions about sentience, moral equality and global justice. The environmental impacts of food systems are complex, but on average show clear benefits for many plant-based diets. A convincing ethical position should take into account both the interests of sentient animals and the social and environmental consequences of food production.
