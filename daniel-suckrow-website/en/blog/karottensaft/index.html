Carrot juice: How healthy is it really and what can purple carrots do?

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Carrot juice is considered a classic for good eyes and healthy skin. A glass does indeed contain a remarkable amount of beta-carotene. This article shows what carrot juice can really do, why a dash of oil belongs in it, whether homemade is better and what purple carrots are all about.

What is in carrot juice?

Carrots are among the best sources of beta-carotene. This is the orange pigment that your body converts into vitamin A when needed. You need vitamin A for vision, especially at dusk, for a working immune system and for healthy skin and mucous membranes.

A single 250-millilitre glass often provides several times your daily vitamin A requirement. The exact amount varies depending on variety, cultivation, storage and production. On top of that come potassium, other carotenoids such as alpha-carotene and lutein, and smaller amounts of vitamin K and B vitamins.

You do not need to worry about an overdose. Your body only converts as much beta-carotene into vitamin A as it needs. If you drink a lot of carrot juice over a long period, your skin may turn slightly orange. This is harmless and disappears again when you drink less.

Why a dash of oil belongs in it

Beta-carotene is fat-soluble. Without some fat, your body absorbs only a small part of it. So the rule is: add a few drops of rapeseed or flaxseed oil to the juice, or drink it with a meal that contains some fat, for example bread with nut butter.

Also interesting: your body can absorb more beta-carotene from juice and cooked carrots than from raw carrots. Pressing, blending and heating break open the cell walls, making the pigment more readily available.

Important: Beta-carotene from food is harmless. High-dose beta-carotene supplements, on the other hand, increased the risk of lung cancer in smokers in studies. So it is better to stick to carrots and juice rather than capsules.

What carrot juice can do for eyes and skin

A vitamin A deficiency can lead to night blindness and, in severe cases, damage to the cornea. If you are well supplied, however, extra carrot juice will not make you see more sharply. The story that carrots improve eyesight partly comes from a campaign in the Second World War, by the way. It was meant to conceal that British pilots were flying at night with radar.

Beta-carotene is also important for the skin. It acts as an antioxidant and is stored in the skin. But it is not sun protection. Carrot juice replaces neither sunscreen nor shade or protective clothing.

Pressed yourself or bought?

Freshly pressed juice tastes particularly good and you choose the ingredients yourself. But it is not automatically more nutritious than a good shop-bought juice. Many not-from-concentrate juices also contain plenty of beta-carotene. Heating for preservation largely retains it and even makes it partly more available.

When buying, look for “not from concentrate” and a short ingredients list without added sugar. Some juices contain lemon juice or lactic acid, which is no problem.

One drawback of every type of juice: when juicing, most of the fibre stays behind in the pulp. A glass of carrot juice contains around 9 to 10 grams of natural sugar. Whole carrots or a smoothie are therefore more filling and make blood sugar rise more slowly. I recommend seeing juice as an addition and also eating carrots whole.

Purple carrots: more than just a pretty colour

Purple carrots, by the way, were around long before orange ones. The orange varieties common today were only bred in the Netherlands from the 16th and 17th centuries. Besides beta-carotene, purple carrots also contain anthocyanins. These are the same plant pigments that give blueberries and red cabbage their colour.

Anthocyanins are being researched for their possible benefits for the heart and blood vessels. However, it has not been proven that purple carrots therefore protect against disease. A small study showed that your body absorbs the beta-carotene from purple and orange carrots similarly well. Purple carrots are thus a lovely and colourful addition, but not a must.

Recipe from practice

For two glasses, press or blend six medium carrots, one apple, a thumb-sized piece of ginger and a squeeze of lemon. Add a teaspoon of flaxseed oil and stir well. If you prefer a smoothie, blend everything with a little water instead of juicing. That way the fibre is retained. With purple carrots, the whole thing turns deep red to violet.

Who carrot juice is a good idea for

Carrot juice is a practical way to improve your vitamin A supply. This is particularly interesting on a plant-based diet, because vitamin A is found there only as beta-carotene. People who eat few vegetables also benefit from a glass now and then.

If you need to watch your blood sugar, you should drink juice in small amounts and with a meal. Toddlers do not need juice. For them, water and whole pieces of vegetables are the better choice.

How much makes sense? A small glass of 150 to 200 millilitres a few times a week is quite enough to significantly improve your beta-carotene supply. You do not need to drink litres of it every day. If you like juice, you can also dilute it with water. That way you save sugar and still have the taste.

Conclusion

Carrot juice provides plenty of beta-carotene, which your body converts into vitamin A when needed. With a dash of oil, it can absorb it much better. You should not expect miracles for your eyesight or sun protection. Homemade juice is not automatically healthier than a good not-from-concentrate juice, and purple carrots are a lovely but not essential addition. Ideally, also eat carrots whole, and you will get valuable fibre as well.
