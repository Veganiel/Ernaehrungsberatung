Critical nutrients on a plant-based diet: What you really need to watch

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

A well-planned plant-based diet can be really healthy. “Well-planned” is the decisive part, though. Some nutrients deserve special attention. This article shows you which ones they are, which you can easily cover through food and where a supplement really makes sense.

What “critical” actually means

Critical does not mean that you are automatically lacking something. It means that a nutrient is harder to reach on a purely plant-based diet and that you should therefore plan it consciously. Some nutrients occur in animal foods in larger amounts or in a more available form. For others, we are all often short, no matter how we eat.

The German Nutrition Society (DGE) updated its assessment in 2024. Its conclusion: a well-planned plant-based diet can be health-promoting for healthy adults. The prerequisite is that vitamin B12 is reliably supplemented and that the other critical nutrients are well covered.

The two must-haves: vitamin B12 and iodine

Vitamin B12 is the only nutrient for which there is no way around a supplement. It is made only by bacteria, and plant foods are not a reliable source. 25 to 100 micrograms daily or 1,000 to 2,000 micrograms once or twice a week have proven themselves.

Iodine is an issue for almost everyone in Germany, because our soils contain little of it. Without fish and dairy products, it becomes even scarcer. Adults need about 150 to 200 micrograms per day.

  • Use iodised salt consistently at home. Five grams already provide around 100 micrograms of iodine.
  • With bread and other products, check whether iodised salt was used.
  • If that is not enough, a supplement with 100 to 150 micrograms can make sense. With thyroid conditions, please only after consulting a doctor.

Important: Seaweeds such as kombu, wakame or hijiki can contain extremely large amounts of iodine, often many times the safe daily upper limit. You should avoid products without an exact iodine statement. They are not a reliable, let alone a safe, source of iodine.

Omega-3, vitamin D and protein

You can easily get the plant omega-3 fatty acid ALA from a tablespoon of ground flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts or rapeseed oil. However, the conversion into the long-chain fatty acids EPA and DHA is limited. I therefore recommend an algae oil with about 250 to 500 milligrams of EPA and DHA per day.

Vitamin D is an issue for almost everyone in Germany from October to March, regardless of diet. For many people 1,000 IU daily is enough in winter, and your blood level shows the right dose. For a plant-based supply there is vitamin D3 from lichen.

Protein is rarely a problem on a plant-based diet if you regularly eat pulses, tofu, tempeh, seitan, wholegrains, nuts and seeds. Variety ensures that the protein building blocks complement each other well. With a lot of sport or in older age, it is worth consciously including a protein-rich source at every meal.

Iron, zinc, calcium, selenium and vitamin B2

You can cover these nutrients well through food if you know a few tricks. The overview shows what matters.

Iron and zinc are found in many wholegrain products and pulses. There, however, they are partly bound to phytate, which slows absorption. Soaking, sprouting, fermenting and sourdough break down phytate. Vitamin C from peppers, berries or lemon significantly improves iron absorption.

Do you need a multi-supplement?

A well-designed supplement specifically for plant-based diets can be practical, because it combines B12, iodine, vitamin D and often selenium and zinc in one tablet. It is not necessary, though. What matters is that the doses match your needs and are not unnecessarily high.

I recommend a targeted approach: vitamin B12 always, iodine and vitamin D depending on the situation, plus algae oil. Everything else is best covered through a varied diet.

A practical example

In the morning you have oats with soya drink, berries, flaxseed and a Brazil nut. At lunch a bowl with lentils, spelt grains, peppers and pumpkin seeds. In the evening tofu with broccoli and kale. You also drink a calcium-rich mineral water, cook with iodised salt and take B12, algae oil and vitamin D in winter. With that, all critical nutrients are well covered.

Which blood values make sense

A check once a year gives you peace of mind. Above all, holo-TC for vitamin B12, ferritin for iron stores and 25(OH)D for vitamin D make sense. Depending on the situation, TSH for the thyroid, zinc or the omega-3 index can be added.

Individual support is particularly important during pregnancy and breastfeeding, for children and teenagers, in older age and with chronic illnesses. Here the requirement is higher or mistakes have faster consequences.

Conclusion

A plant-based diet does not automatically lead to a deficiency, but it does need some planning. You always have to supplement vitamin B12. Iodine, omega-3 and vitamin D deserve special attention. You can cover iron, zinc, calcium, selenium and vitamin B2 well with pulses, wholegrains, nuts, seeds, vegetables and a few simple tricks. A yearly blood check shows you whether everything is on track.
