Omega-3 and omega-6: Which ratio is good and what really matters?

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Omega-6 is often seen as the “bad” fat and omega-3 as the “good” one. On top of that, figures such as the ideal ratio of 4 to 1 are going around. Reality is simpler and more relaxed. This article shows why you need both fatty acids, what is really known about inflammation and how to be well supplied in everyday life.

Two fatty acids you need both of

Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids belong to the polyunsaturated fatty acids. One of each is essential, meaning your body cannot make it itself:

  • Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) is the plant omega-3 fatty acid. It is found in flaxseed, chia seeds, hemp seeds, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
  • Linoleic acid is the most important omega-6 fatty acid. It is found in almost all nuts, seeds and vegetable oils.

From ALA your body can make the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA, but only in small amounts. DHA in particular is important for the brain and eyes. Linoleic acid is turned into arachidonic acid, among other things, which is a normal part of every cell membrane.

Is omega-6 inflammatory?

You hear this statement very often. But it is too simple. Omega-6 fatty acids do give rise to messenger substances that are involved in inflammation. Other messengers from the same source, however, help to resolve inflammation again. Your body needs both.

Studies also show that people who eat more linoleic acid do not automatically have more arachidonic acid in their blood or higher inflammation markers. On the contrary, in large observational studies a higher intake of linoleic acid is linked to a slightly lower risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes.

So omega-6 is not the enemy. The real problem is rather a diet with lots of highly processed products, fried food and few vegetables. These do contain a lot of omega-6, but the actual problem is the overall package.

How important is the ratio?

A ratio of omega-6 to omega-3 of 5 to 1 or even lower is often recommended. The DGE long gave a value of 5 to 1 as a guide. But the ratio alone has a catch: it says nothing about whether you get enough of both fatty acids.

Both fatty acids partly use the same enzymes. A very high amount of linoleic acid can therefore slow the conversion of ALA into EPA and DHA somewhat. What matters more, though, is that you take in enough omega-3. Instead of anxiously avoiding omega-6, it makes more sense to strengthen the omega-3 side.

Important: A good ratio develops almost by itself if you eat an omega-3 source every day and mainly use rapeseed and olive oil. You do not have to calculate an exact number for this.

Flaxseed oil is sensitive to light, heat and oxygen. Keep it in the fridge, use it up within a few weeks and do not heat it. You should eat flaxseed ground, otherwise it often passes through the gut undigested.

Five simple ideas for more omega-3

With these small habits you can bring omega-3 into your everyday life along the way:

  • Stir a tablespoon of ground flaxseed into your muesli, porridge or yoghurt.
  • Make a chia pudding with plant drink and berries for breakfast or dessert.
  • Sprinkle walnuts or hemp seeds over salads, bowls and roasted vegetables.
  • Use rapeseed oil as your standard oil for cooking and frying at medium heat.
  • Mix a salad dressing from flaxseed oil, lemon, mustard and herbs. Only add the oil shortly before serving.

At the same time, try to cut down on highly processed snacks, fried food and ready meals. That way you improve not only your fatty acid profile, but your whole diet.

EPA and DHA: When does algae oil make sense?

Fish provides EPA and DHA directly. But it only got them from microalgae in the first place. If you do not eat fish, you can therefore go straight to the source: algae oil.

Because conversion from ALA is limited, on a plant-based diet I recommend an algae oil with about 250 to 500 milligrams of EPA and DHA per day. EFSA gives 250 milligrams as an adequate intake for adults. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, 100 to 200 milligrams of DHA are added.

A practical example

In the morning you eat oats with a tablespoon of ground flaxseed and you cook with rapeseed oil. That covers your ALA supply well. In addition, you take one algae oil capsule with 300 milligrams of EPA and DHA every day. If you want to know exactly, have your omega-3 index measured in the blood after three to four months. Values around 8 per cent are considered favourable.

What omega-3 can and cannot do

It is well established that high-dose EPA and DHA can lower blood fats, more precisely triglycerides. It is also healthy to replace saturated fats with unsaturated fats. This lowers LDL cholesterol and thus the risk of cardiovascular disease.

Be careful with big promises. Omega-3 capsules do not cure rheumatism, osteoarthritis or depression. For some conditions there are positive signs, but the evidence is mixed. A supplement does not replace treatment.

Conclusion

Omega-3 and omega-6 are both important. Instead of demonising omega-6, you should above all eat enough omega-3. Flaxseed, chia seeds or walnuts every day and rapeseed or olive oil in the kitchen ensure a good ratio along the way. If you do not eat fish, algae oil with 250 to 500 milligrams of EPA and DHA is a useful addition. You do not need to calculate an exact ratio for this.
