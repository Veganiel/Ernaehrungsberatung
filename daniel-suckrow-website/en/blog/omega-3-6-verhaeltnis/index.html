Omega-3 and Omega-6: Ratio, Sources and What Really Matters

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids are essential polyunsaturated fatty acids. Both fulfill important functions in the body. Their relationship to one another is often discussed. However, research shows that it is not a single ratio that determines health, but rather the absolute intake, the food sources and the overall dietary pattern.

The 1x1 of fatty acids

Fatty acids can be divided into saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids. The polyunsaturated fatty acids include the omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids.

The essential omega-3 fatty acid alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) is found, for example, in flaxseeds, chia seeds, walnuts and rapeseed oil. The body can form a limited amount of the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid) from ALA. The conversion varies from person to person and the formation of DHA in particular is low.

The important omega-6 fatty acids include linoleic acid and arachidonic acid. Linoleic acid is essential and is found, for example, in sunflower, corn germ and soybean oil as well as in nuts and seeds. The body can produce arachidonic acid from linoleic acid and it is also absorbed through animal foods.

Are omega-6 fatty acids pro-inflammatory?

Omega-6 fatty acids are often generally described as pro-inflammatory. That's too simplistic. Linoleic acid and arachidonic acid are involved in the formation of various messenger substances that can have both pro-inflammatory and inflammation-resolving or regulating functions. Arachidonic acid is a normal component of cell membranes and is important for numerous body functions.

A higher intake of linoleic acid does not automatically lead to a strong increase in arachidonic acid or to chronic inflammation in humans. Studies also do not consistently show that higher linoleic acid intake worsens inflammatory markers. Omega-6 fatty acids are therefore not fundamentally unhealthy and should not be avoided completely.

The ratio of omega-3 to omega-6 is important - but not alone

In nutritional discussions, a certain omega-6 to omega-3 ratio is often described as optimal. However, frequently cited numbers such as 4:1 or 5:1 are not a generally accepted individual target. A ratio can also be misleading: it does not say whether both fatty acids are consumed in sufficient quantities.

Omega-6 and omega-3 fatty acids use some of the same enzyme systems. High linoleic acid intake can therefore influence the conversion of ALA into EPA and DHA. However, the actual conversion depends on several factors and cannot be predicted by the ratio alone. Gender, metabolism and other nutritional factors also play a role.

It makes more sense than following an exact ratio to consume enough ALA, regularly choose sources of unsaturated fatty acids and keep your diet varied. Very large amounts of individual oils do not have to be consumed for this.

EPA and DHA: Is algae oil useful?

EPA and DHA perform important tasks, including as components of cell membranes. DHA is particularly present in the brain and retina. Although ALA can be converted into EPA and DHA, the conversion rate is limited. Algae oil provides a direct plant source of EPA and/or DHA, making it a practical option for people who don't eat fish.

There is not a uniform, binding daily EPA and DHA target amount for healthy adults in all countries. Care should be viewed in the context of overall nutrition and individual life situations. DHA is particularly relevant during pregnancy and breastfeeding; recommendations may differ from those for the general population. A general daily intake of 450 mg DHA plus 250 mg EPA is therefore not a general requirement.

Even the frequently mentioned omega-3 index of 8 percent is not a universally set limit that means a guaranteed protective effect against cardiovascular diseases for every person. It is a research and potential risk marker whose clinical application continues to be debated.

Health Benefits: What's Proven?

Replacing saturated fatty acids with polyunsaturated fatty acids can lower LDL cholesterol and have a beneficial effect on cardiovascular risk. There is also evidence of certain health effects for omega-3 fatty acids; However, these differ depending on the fatty acid, dose, group of people and endpoint examined.

This does not mean that a high omega-3 intake generally prevents or treats inflammation, rheumatism, osteoarthritis or depression. The study situation on sleep and eye diseases also varies depending on the question. Dietary supplements should therefore not be seen as a replacement for medical treatment.

Which fats should you prefer in everyday life?

Saturated fatty acids do not have to be specifically consumed through food. Sustained high intake can increase LDL cholesterol, especially if it displaces unsaturated fatty acids. The WHO recommends limiting saturated fatty acids and preferably replacing them with polyunsaturated fatty acids, plant-based monounsaturated fatty acids or foods rich in fiber.

For example, rapeseed oil and olive oil are suitable versatile kitchen oils for everyday use. Flax seeds, chia seeds and walnuts provide ALA. Sunflower oil doesn't have to be avoided completely: it provides linoleic acid, an essential omega-6 fatty acid. Coconut oil, on the other hand, is rich in saturated fatty acids and should not be used as a preferred substitute for unsaturated vegetable oils.

Practical tips for a good supply of Omega-3

  • Regularly incorporate flaxseeds, chia seeds, walnuts or rapeseed oil into your diet as sources of ALA.
  • Crushed flaxseeds may be more accessible than whole seeds. Flaxseed and hemp oil are sensitive to light, heat and oxygen. Store them in a cool, dark and well-sealed place and do not use them for searing.
  • Use rapeseed or olive oil as versatile kitchen oils. There is no need to avoid all omega-6 rich oils.
  • If you follow a vegan diet or don't eat fish, an algae oil with declared EPA and DHA content can be a practical supplement. Check the actual amount per daily serving and the quality of the product.
  • During pregnancy and breastfeeding, DHA supply should be discussed individually with a medical professional.

Conclusion

Omega-3 and Omega-6 fatty acids are both important. The lowest possible omega-6 to omega-3 ratio is not automatically the goal. What is crucial is an adequate supply of essential fatty acids, suitable food sources and the replacement of saturated with unsaturated fats. For people who don't eat fish, algae oil is a direct plant source of EPA and DHA.
