Omega-3, vitamin D and vitamin C: Which supplements really make sense?

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

The range of supplements is huge, and you do not need most of it. In this article I take a closer look at three classics: omega-3 from algae oil, vitamin D and vitamin C. You will learn when they make sense, which amounts have proven themselves and where you can save your money.

Diet first, then the supplement

A supplement can complement a good diet, but not replace it. You get many nutrients in sufficient amounts from vegetables, fruit, pulses, wholegrains, nuts and seeds. More is not automatically better. Some vitamins can even do harm in high doses.

A supplement mainly makes sense when a nutrient is hard to get through your diet or lifestyle. That is often exactly the case with omega-3 and vitamin D. Vitamin C is a different story.

Omega-3: Why algae oil can be a good idea

With omega-3 you need to distinguish between two groups. The plant fatty acid ALA is found in flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts and rapeseed oil. The long-chain fatty acids EPA and DHA, on the other hand, are mainly found in oily fish and in microalgae. The fish, by the way, only got them from the algae in the first place.

Your body can convert ALA into EPA and DHA, but only in small amounts. For DHA the conversion is often in the low single-digit percentage range. Yet EPA and DHA play an important role for the brain, the eyes and the heart in particular.

  • EFSA gives 250 milligrams of EPA and DHA per day as an adequate intake for adults.
  • During pregnancy and breastfeeding, an extra 100 to 200 milligrams of DHA are added.
  • One tablespoon of ground flaxseed or a handful of walnuts covers your ALA needs well.

If you do not eat fish, I recommend an algae oil with about 250 to 500 milligrams of EPA and DHA per day. Check the actual amount per daily serving and choose a product that is tested for contaminants and oxidation. Store the oil somewhere cool and dark.

If you want to know exactly, you can have your omega-3 index measured in the blood. It shows the share of EPA and DHA in the red blood cells. Values around 8 per cent are often considered favourable.

Vitamin D: Almost always an issue in winter

Your body mainly makes vitamin D itself when the sun shines on your skin. From October to March, however, the sun is too low in Germany for this. Many people therefore have low levels by the end of winter.

The DGE gives 800 IU (20 micrograms) per day if the body makes no vitamin D itself. In practice, 1,000 IU daily is enough for many people in winter. People who rarely go outside often need 2,000 IU or more. Your blood level is the best guide to the dose that suits you.

Important: You should not take high single doses such as 60,000 IU per week on your own initiative. EFSA has set a tolerable upper intake level of 4,000 IU per day for adults. Too much vitamin D can raise the calcium level in the blood dangerously.

You will find everything important about sun, dosage, blood levels and vitamin K2 in my detailed article on vitamin D in winter.

Vitamin C: Your diet is usually enough

You need vitamin C for your immune system, for making collagen and for absorbing iron from plant foods, among other things. Humans cannot make it themselves.

The good news: you can easily reach the requirement of about 95 to 110 milligrams per day with fruit and vegetables. Half a red pepper, a portion of broccoli or a kiwi with an orange already provide enough. Smokers have a higher requirement.

High-dose supplements usually bring no additional benefit. Whatever goes beyond your needs is largely excreted again. With colds, regular intake can shorten the duration slightly. According to studies, people who only start at the first symptoms hardly benefit. Very high amounts can also cause digestive complaints.

A practical example

You eat at least three portions of vegetables and two portions of fruit every day. Then you are well supplied with vitamin C and can save yourself a supplement. For iron, though, one tip is worth its weight in gold: eat some pepper, berries or a squeeze of lemon with lentils or oats. That way your body absorbs plant iron much better.

How to recognise a good supplement

In supermarkets, chemists and online you will find countless products. These points help you choose:

  • The amount per daily serving is clearly stated, for omega-3 separately for EPA and DHA.
  • The dose matches your needs and is not far above them.
  • There are transparent tests for purity and contaminants, ideally by an independent lab.
  • The ingredients list is short. You do not need unnecessary additives, colourings or sugar.
  • For a plant-based supply, the product is labelled accordingly, for example vitamin D3 from lichen.

Expensive complexes with dozens of ingredients are rarely necessary. It often makes more sense to supplement only the few nutrients you really need.

Conclusion

Not every supplement makes sense, but some certainly do. If you do not eat fish, an algae oil with 250 to 500 milligrams of EPA and DHA is a good addition. Vitamin D is an issue for most people from October to March, and your blood level shows the right dose. Vitamin C, on the other hand, you can easily get yourself from fruit and vegetables. That way you invest specifically where it pays off.
