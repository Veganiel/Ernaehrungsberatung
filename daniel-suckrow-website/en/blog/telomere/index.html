Telomeres and telomerase: Can you influence how your cells age?

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Telomeres are seen as a kind of biological clock in our cells. With every year they get a little shorter. No wonder they get so much attention in the longevity field. This article explains what telomeres and telomerase are, how much your lifestyle really matters and why anti-ageing supplements do not deliver what they promise.

What are Telomeres?

In every cell, your genetic material is spread across 46 chromosomes. Telomeres sit at their ends. They consist of thousands of repeats of the same short DNA sequence and protect the chromosomes much like the plastic tips at the ends of shoelaces. Without them, the chromosomes would fray, stick together or be wrongly recognised by the cell as damage.

With every cell division, the cell cannot fully copy the outermost ends of the DNA. In many cells the telomeres therefore get a little shorter with each division. When they reach a critical length, the cell stops dividing. It goes into a kind of retirement or dies.

At first, this is a protective mechanism. It prevents cells with damaged genetic material from multiplying uncontrollably. At the same time, it contributes to tissues renewing themselves less well in old age.

Telomerase: the enzyme that lengthens telomeres

Telomerase is an enzyme that can lengthen telomeres again. It brings its own template in the form of a short piece of RNA and uses it to attach new DNA building blocks to the chromosome ends. Elizabeth Blackburn, Carol Greider and Jack Szostak received the 2009 Nobel Prize in Medicine for discovering this mechanism.

Telomerase is mainly active in germ cells, stem cells and some immune cells. In most mature body cells, on the other hand, it is hardly active at all. That sounds like a disadvantage at first, but there is a good reason for it.

Important: Around 85 to 90 per cent of all cancer cells switch telomerase back on. This allows them to divide almost without limit. So more telomerase is not automatically better. Activating it across the board would not be a harmless anti-ageing goal, but could in the worst case increase the risk of cancer.

Are short telomeres a sign of rapid ageing?

Telomere length is often used as a marker of biological age. In large studies, people with shorter telomeres have, on average, a slightly higher risk of cardiovascular disease and other age-related illnesses.

For you as an individual, however, a telomere test is not very meaningful. Length varies greatly between people, is largely inherited and fluctuates depending on the measurement method and cell type. A test result therefore says little about how healthy you are or how old you will get. In my view, you can save yourself commercial telomere tests.

How much does your lifestyle matter?

This is where it gets exciting. Various studies find links between lifestyle and telomere length:

  • Diet: In observational studies, a diet with plenty of vegetables, fruit, pulses, wholegrains and nuts, as well as the Mediterranean diet, go hand in hand with longer telomeres. A lot of sugar, sausage and highly processed foods tend to go with shorter ones.
  • Exercise: Physically active people have, on average, longer telomeres than inactive people, especially with regular endurance training.
  • Stress: A well-known study found significantly shorter telomeres in mothers caring for a chronically ill child than in less stressed mothers.
  • Smoking, being overweight and lack of sleep are linked to shorter telomeres.

A small pilot study by Dean Ornish caused a stir in 2013. Men who ate a plant-focused diet, exercised regularly and practised stress management for five years had, on average, longer telomeres afterwards than at the start. With only ten participants in the lifestyle group, however, the study was very small. It provides an interesting hint, but not yet proof.

The context matters: most results come from observational studies. They show associations, but no clear cause. Even so, one thing is clear: these habits are good for you for many other reasons. Healthier telomeres would be a nice extra bonus.

What about supplements that claim to lengthen telomeres?

Numerous products are offered online that are supposed to activate telomerase or lengthen telomeres. Typical ingredients are extracts of astragalus and schisandra, resveratrol, curcumin or combinations with vitamin D and omega-3.

For none of these supplements has it been proven that they lengthen telomeres in humans in a health-relevant way or slow down ageing. Most of the evidence comes from cell and animal experiments. Given the role of telomerase in cancer, targeted activation is also not without risk. Better to save your money and invest it in good food or a new pair of running shoes.

What you can do in practice

A practical example

You want to age healthily in the long term. Instead of an expensive telomere test and anti-ageing capsules, you focus on five basics: a colourful, plant-focused diet with pulses and wholegrains every day, at least 150 minutes of exercise per week, seven to eight hours of sleep, a regular relaxation routine such as an evening walk, and no tobacco. These habits have been shown to lower your risk of many diseases, whatever they change in your telomeres.

Tip: Start with one habit that comes easily to you and build on it. Small steps you keep up in the long run achieve more than big resolutions that are forgotten after three weeks.

Conclusion

Telomeres protect the ends of your chromosomes and get shorter with age. Telomerase can lengthen them, but it is also used by cancer cells. So more is not automatically better here. In studies, a healthy lifestyle with a plant-focused diet, exercise, good sleep and little constant stress is linked to longer telomeres. Supplements that claim to lengthen telomeres, on the other hand, are not backed by evidence. Focus on the basics, they pay off either way.
