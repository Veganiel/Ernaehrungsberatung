Dealing with stress: What happens in your body and what really helps

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Everyone knows stress: before an exam, during an argument or when the tasks pile up. In the short term it is even useful. But when it becomes a permanent state, it takes its toll on sleep, mood, digestion and the heart. This article shows you what happens in your body under stress, how to tell when it is becoming too much and which strategies have been shown to help in everyday life.

What happens in your body under stress

Stress is an ancient survival response. When your brain perceives a challenge or danger, your body switches into alarm mode within seconds. Adrenaline and noradrenaline make your heart and breathing speed up, your muscles tense and sugar is released into the blood. A little later the stress hormone cortisol follows. It keeps energy available for longer.

At the same time, your body shuts down everything that is not vital at that moment: digestion, repair and recovery. For a short sprint away from a predator, that was perfect. For a full email inbox, it is rather unsuitable.

By the way, whether a situation triggers stress depends greatly on how you assess it. The same presentation can be an exciting challenge for one person and a sleepless night for another.

When stress becomes a problem

Short bursts of stress followed by recovery are normal and can even make you more capable. It becomes a problem when recovery is missing. Then the body stays on permanent alert.

Typical warning signs are:

  • You have trouble falling asleep or wake up at night with racing thoughts.
  • You are often irritable, tense or restless inside.
  • You have headaches, a stiff neck or digestive complaints.
  • You find it harder to concentrate and forget more than usual.
  • Things that used to bring you joy now only feel exhausting.

Chronic stress is linked to a higher risk of high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, depression and anxiety disorders. It weakens the immune system and often leads to unhelpful habits such as snacking, more alcohol and less exercise.

Important: If you have felt exhausted, low or overwhelmed for weeks, get support from your GP or a psychotherapy practice. In an acute crisis in Germany, you can reach the TelefonSeelsorge free of charge around the clock on 0800 111 0 111.

The three levels of stress

A helpful model from stress research distinguishes three levels. You can start at each of them:

  • The triggers: deadline pressure, conflicts, noise, caring responsibilities or money worries. Some can be changed, others cannot.
  • Your inner amplifiers: thoughts such as “I have to be perfect”, “I must not disappoint anyone” or “I have to manage everything on my own”.
  • Your response: what happens in your body and feelings and how you recover afterwards.

Often the biggest lever lies with the inner amplifiers. In a stressful situation, ask yourself: What exactly am I expecting of myself right now? Is that realistic? What would I advise a good friend in the same situation?

Immediate help: breathing and movement

When stress is acute, it helps to start directly with the body. Two strategies are particularly effective:

The first is a simple breathing exercise called cyclic sighing. Breathe in deeply through your nose, add a short second breath on top and then breathe out slowly and fully through your mouth. Repeat this for five minutes. In a Stanford University study, this exercise improved mood and calm even more than mindfulness meditation of the same length. The long exhale activates the part of the nervous system responsible for rest and recovery.

The second is movement. Even a brisk ten-minute walk breaks down stress hormones and lifts your mood. Regular exercise makes you more relaxed about new pressures in the long run.

Calmer in the long term: habits that strengthen you

  • Sleep: seven to nine hours are the foundation. Fixed times and an hour without screens before bed help.
  • Breaks: build short breaks firmly into your day, ideally outdoors.
  • Boundaries: practise saying no and delegating tasks. Not everything is equally urgent.
  • Social contact: conversations with people you trust are one of the strongest protective factors against stress.
  • Relaxation techniques: progressive muscle relaxation, yoga or meditation work better the more regularly you practise them.

What nutrition can contribute under stress

Under stress, many people reach for sweets, fast food or more coffee. That is understandable, but it often makes things worse. Strong blood sugar swings and too much caffeine can increase restlessness and irritability.

  • Eat regularly and combine carbohydrates with protein and fibre, for example oats with nuts or wholegrain bread with hummus.
  • Limit coffee to two or three cups and do not drink it in the afternoon.
  • Go easy on alcohol. It helps you fall asleep but worsens sleep quality.
  • Make sure you get enough magnesium, B vitamins and omega-3, for example from pulses, wholegrains, nuts, green vegetables and flaxseed.

A practical example

You notice that during a stressful project phase you only unwind in the evening with crisps and series, and you sleep badly. You start with three small changes: five minutes of cyclic sighing in the morning, a ten-minute walk at lunchtime and no more coffee after 2 pm. After two weeks you fall asleep faster and feel less rushed during the day.

Conclusion

Stress is a natural response that makes you capable in the short term. It becomes a problem when recovery is missing. Watch out for warning signs such as poor sleep, irritability and exhaustion. Question your inner drivers, use breathing exercises and movement as immediate help, and build sleep, breaks and social contact as firm pillars. A regular, balanced diet supports you in this. And if you do not feel better over a longer period, get professional help.
