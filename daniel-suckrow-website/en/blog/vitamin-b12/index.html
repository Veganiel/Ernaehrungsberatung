Vitamin B12 on a plant-based diet: How much do you really need?

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Vitamin B12 is the one nutrient on a plant-based diet that is not up for debate: you need a reliable source. This article shows why unwashed vegetables or algae are not enough, which doses have proven themselves and which blood values let you check your status safely.

Where vitamin B12 actually comes from

Vitamin B12 is made neither by plants nor by animals, but only by certain bacteria. Animals take it in through their feed or from bacteria in their gut. In modern livestock farming, vitamin B12 is in fact often added to the feed.

On a plant-based diet, this source is missing. You often hear that soil on unwashed vegetables, sauerkraut or algae such as nori or chlorella would be enough. You should not rely on that. The amounts vary widely, and many of these foods mainly contain B12 analogues. These are compounds that look like vitamin B12 in the lab but do not work in the body.

The body can store B12 in the liver for several years. A deficiency therefore often develops gradually and is noticed late. That is exactly what makes it treacherous.

Why a deficiency is so serious

Vitamin B12 is needed for blood formation, cell division and a healthy nervous system. A long-standing deficiency can lead to tiredness, poor concentration, tingling in the hands and feet or anaemia. Nerve damage can remain if the deficiency goes untreated for too long.

A reliable supply is especially important during pregnancy, while breastfeeding and in children. Here a deficiency has faster and stronger effects than in adults.

By the way, B12 deficiency does not only affect people who eat plant-based. Older people, people with stomach or bowel conditions and people who take acid blockers or metformin long term are also often undersupplied.

How much vitamin B12 makes sense?

The German Nutrition Society (DGE) gives an estimated value of 4 micrograms per day for adults. This value describes the requirement, however, not the amount in a supplement. The reason: per meal, the body can only absorb about 1.5 to 2 micrograms through a special transport route. Of anything above that, only around 1 per cent additionally reaches the blood.

That is why the doses in supplements are much higher. In practice, two approaches have proven themselves:

  • 25 to 100 micrograms daily, for example as a tablet or drops.
  • 1,000 to 2,000 micrograms once or twice a week.
  • Fortified foods such as plant drinks can help as well, but usually do not replace a supplement.

I recommend taking it daily, because that is the easiest way to make it a routine. If you often forget, the weekly option is a good choice.

Important: The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has not set an upper limit for vitamin B12, as no harmful effects of high intakes are known. If you already have a deficiency, the normal maintenance dose is often not enough. Topping up should then be supervised by a doctor, sometimes with injections.

Which form should you choose?

In shops you will mainly find cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin, hydroxocobalamin and adenosylcobalamin. Cyanocobalamin is the best studied, very stable and inexpensive. For most healthy people it is a good choice.

Methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin are the forms the body uses itself. They are somewhat less stable. If you prefer a combination, you can choose a supplement with several forms. For people with reduced kidney function or smokers, hydroxo- or methylcobalamin is often advised.

Tip: Lozenges and drops can also be absorbed through the lining of the mouth. What matters most, though, is that you take your supplement regularly.

Which blood values show your status?

The usual serum B12 value is unfortunately not very meaningful. It can still be normal even though the cells are already getting too little. Holotranscobalamin, or holo-TC for short, is better suited. It shows the B12 that is actually available to the cells. Methylmalonic acid (MMA), on the other hand, only rises once there is already a deficiency in the cells.

Have your values checked once a year, ideally holo-TC and MMA if needed. Do not take your supplement the day before, so the result is not distorted.

A practical example

You have been eating plant-based for two years and take a B12 supplement irregularly. Your holo-TC is 38 pmol/l, so in the grey area. From now on you take 50 micrograms daily and have the value checked again after three months. If it is then above 50 pmol/l, you stay with this dose.

Three common misconceptions

  • “I eat lots of organic vegetables, there is enough B12 on them.” Even if traces are present, the amount is far too small and unpredictable.
  • “My serum B12 is normal, so everything is fine.” The value can look normal even though the stores are empty. Holo-TC is more meaningful.
  • “I don't have any symptoms.” A deficiency often develops over years without you noticing. Early signs such as tiredness are easy to miss.

Conclusion

Only bacteria make vitamin B12. On a plant-based diet you therefore need a supplement or enough fortified foods. Unwashed vegetables, algae or fermented foods are not a reliable source. 25 to 100 micrograms daily or 1,000 to 2,000 micrograms once or twice a week have proven themselves. Have your status checked once a year via holo-TC. That way you are on the safe side, even though a deficiency can go unnoticed for a long time.
