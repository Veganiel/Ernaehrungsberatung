Less salt: How to keep your food really tasty

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

Most people eat almost twice as much salt as recommended. The good news: less salt does not mean less flavour. This article shows why cutting down is worth it, where most salt is hidden and which simple kitchen tricks make your food taste really good even with less salt.

Why less salt is worth it

Table salt consists mainly of sodium chloride. Your body needs sodium, for example for fluid balance and the nerves. But the amount needed is small. In many people, eating too much salt over the long term raises blood pressure. And high blood pressure is one of the most important risk factors for stroke, heart attack and kidney disease.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that adults eat less than 5 grams of salt a day. That is about one level teaspoon, from all sources combined. The German Nutrition Society gives up to 6 grams as a guide. In reality, men in Germany eat around 10 grams on average and women just over 8 grams a day.

The good news: even a reduction of a few grams can measurably lower blood pressure. The effect is particularly clear in people who already have raised values.

Where most salt is hidden

Around three quarters of salt does not come from the salt shaker, but from processed foods. Bread, ready meals, cheese, sausage, snacks and sauces top the list. The table shows some examples.

A look at the nutrition label helps. As a rule of thumb, more than 1.5 grams of salt per 100 grams counts as high, less than 0.3 grams as low.

Seven kitchen tricks for more flavour

Your sense of taste gets used to less salt within about three to six weeks. After that, heavily salted food often even tastes unpleasantly salty. These tricks help you make the switch:

  • Herbs and spices: basil, parsley, thyme, rosemary, cumin, paprika, curry, chilli, garlic and ginger bring plenty of aroma. Check the ingredients of spice blends, as some consist largely of salt.
  • Acidity: a squeeze of lemon, lime or vinegar makes other flavours stand out more. It works almost like a pinch of salt.
  • Roasting: roasted vegetables, browned onions and briefly dry-toasted spices taste more intense and need less salt.
  • Umami: tomato purée, sun-dried tomatoes, mushrooms such as shiitake and nutritional yeast give dishes a savoury depth.
  • Heat: pepper, chilli, mustard or horseradish steer the taste and make food seem more flavourful.
  • Salt at the end: you taste salt on the surface more strongly. If you only season at the end, less is enough.
  • Cooking water: you can cook pasta, spelt grains and potatoes without salt or with only a little. The seasoning comes from the sauce.

Important: Soy sauce, miso, yeast extract, stock cubes and many fermented foods do bring umami, but they contain a lot of salt themselves. Use them sparingly or choose reduced-salt versions.

Shop smart and eat out wisely

With a few shopping habits you can save a lot of salt without changing anything in the kitchen:

  • Compare similar products. The salt content of bread, stock or meat alternatives often differs by a factor of two.
  • Buy tinned pulses and vegetables without added salt. You can rinse salted versions under running water. This significantly reduces the salt content.
  • Cook from fresh ingredients more often. That way you decide the amount.
  • In restaurants you can order sauces and dressings on the side and ask for less salt.

A practical example

You are cooking a lentil curry. Instead of a stock cube, you fry onions, garlic, ginger and cumin well and add a tablespoon of tomato purée. After cooking, you season with lime juice, fresh coriander and a small pinch of iodised salt. The result: an aromatic curry with about a third of the salt you would otherwise have used.

Iodised salt and salt substitutes

If you use salt, iodised salt is best. Iodine is often scarce in Germany, especially on a plant-based diet. So less salt means: less, but iodised. Expensive speciality salts such as Himalayan or sea salt offer no health benefits and usually contain no added iodine.

Salt substitutes with potassium chloride can be an option for healthy adults and lower blood pressure further. People with kidney disease or on medicines that affect potassium levels, such as certain blood pressure drugs, should only use such products after consulting a doctor.

Conclusion

Most people eat considerably more salt than the recommended 5 to 6 grams a day. Most of it is in bread, ready-made products, cheese, sausage and sauces. With herbs, acidity, roasting, umami and a little heat, your food tastes really good even with less salt. Your taste gets used to it after a few weeks. If you add salt, use iodised salt and ideally only at the end.
