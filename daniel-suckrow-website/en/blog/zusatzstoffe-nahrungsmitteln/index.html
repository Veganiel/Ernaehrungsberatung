Food additives: Which E numbers you should keep an eye on

By Daniel Suckrow · Holistic nutritional advice

A glance at the ingredients list is often enough to make you uneasy: emulsifiers, stabilisers, colourings and a whole series of E numbers. Are they all dangerous? No, but not all of them are completely harmless either. This article shows how additives are tested, which ones you should keep an eye on and why the way you eat matters more than individual numbers.

What E numbers mean

Additives are added to foods for technical reasons. They make them last longer, give them colour, create a creamy texture or stop water and oil from separating. Every additive approved in the EU gets an E number. The “E” stands for Europe.

An E number is therefore not a warning sign, but an identifier. E300, for example, is simply vitamin C, E330 is citric acid and E948 is oxygen. Many additives also occur naturally in foods.

Before an additive is approved, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) checks its safety. For many substances it sets an acceptable daily intake, or ADI for short. This amount is considered safe even if you take it in every day for your whole life. When new findings emerge, they are reassessed. Titanium dioxide (E171), for example, has been banned in the EU since 2022.

Which additives deserve attention

Most additives are unproblematic in the permitted amounts. For some, however, there is new research or there are special warnings. The overview shows the most important ones.

Research on emulsifiers such as carboxymethylcellulose (E466) or polysorbate 80 (E433) is particularly interesting. In animal experiments and a small human study, they altered the gut flora and the protective mucus layer in the gut. Large observational studies from France also found links between a high intake of certain emulsifiers and cardiovascular disease. That is not yet proof, but a good reason not to eat them in large amounts every day.

Important: If you react sensitively to certain additives, for example to sulphites (E220 to E228) with asthma or to benzoates, you should read the ingredients list carefully. If you suspect an intolerance, it is worth getting it checked by a doctor.

The cocktail effect

Additives are tested individually. In everyday life, however, you take in many different substances at the same time. Whether they can amplify each other is a legitimate question. Research on this is still in its early stages. EFSA is developing methods to better assess such combined effects.

This does not mean that every combination is dangerous. But it is a further argument for not making highly processed foods with long ingredients lists the basis of your diet.

The real problem: highly processed foods

Studies show again and again that people who eat a lot of highly processed foods have a higher risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and depression. These include ready meals, soft drinks, sweets, crisps, sausage and many breakfast cereals.

Additives are probably only part of the explanation. Such products often contain a lot of sugar, salt and unfavourable fats, but little fibre and few nutrients. They are designed so that it is easy to eat too much of them. And they push fresh food off the plate.

That is why my recommendation is: focus less on individual E numbers and more on the overall picture. The more you cook from fresh ingredients, the less you need to worry about additives.

How helpful are apps like Yuka?

Apps like Yuka scan the barcode and rate a product with a score. With Yuka, nutritional quality accounts for about 60 per cent, additives for 30 per cent and whether the product is organic for 10 per cent. That can be a good guide when shopping, especially for comparing similar products.

But you should not take the ratings too strictly. Some additives are rated more harshly than the evidence warrants. And a good score does not make a highly processed product a healthy food. A fresh apple does not need a scan.

A practical example

You are standing in the supermarket in front of the shelf with plant drinks. One contains oats, water, rapeseed oil, salt and calcium. Another also contains sugar, flavourings, two emulsifiers and gellan. Both are safe, but the first is the better choice. The rule of thumb: the shorter the ingredients list and the more ingredients you recognise, the better.

Five simple rules for shopping

  • Buy as many unprocessed foods as possible, such as vegetables, fruit, pulses, wholegrains, nuts and seeds.
  • Compare similar products and choose the one with the shorter ingredients list.
  • Eat sausage and cured products rarely. With plant-based cold cuts, it is worth checking additives and salt content.
  • For children’s sweets, choose products without azo dyes.
  • Make ready-made products the exception and cook for yourself as often as possible.

Conclusion

E numbers are no reason to panic. Additives are tested in the EU and reassessed or banned when new findings emerge. Some still deserve a critical look, such as certain emulsifiers, nitrite in sausage and azo dyes in children’s sweets. Even more important than individual substances, though, is how many highly processed foods you eat overall. Cook fresh as often as possible, and the question of additives largely takes care of itself.
